The Cost Baseline

Work out what a process costs today, what it would cost automated, and what the difference actually is. Most of the gap between those last two is made of things people forget to count.

Why proposals overstate the saving

Nobody is lying. The number is produced the obvious way — count the hours the process takes, multiply by a rate, multiply by the share you expect to automate — and every step of that is reasonable on its own. What it misses is that automation moves work rather than deleting it, and that hours saved are not the same thing as money saved.

What gets claimed

“This saves us 400 hours a year”

Usually true, usually irrelevant. Hours are not a budget line. Nobody writes a cheque for an hour.

What gets asked

“So what changes?”

Does somebody leave? Does a role change? Do we handle more? If the answer is “things are nicer,” say so — it is a real benefit and a different argument.

A case that survives scrutiny is one where you got to the uncomfortable number before the finance director did.

Build it one step at a time

Start with the defaults — a support queue of eighty items a week — or put your own process in straight away. Each step adds one piece and recalculates, so you can watch the claimed saving shrink toward the real one.

Run your own numbers

Everything unlocked. Change one input at a time and watch which one moves the answer most — that is the input worth measuring properly rather than guessing.

The one thing to carry

Hours saved are not money saved. The business case is the sentence that says which one you are claiming, and what has to be true for it to happen.
