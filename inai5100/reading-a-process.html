Reading a Process

Anyone can be taught to click through a setup screen. The skill that lasts is knowing which process is worth automating — and which step inside it should stay human. This page gives you the vocabulary. The casebook is where you practice it.

Three questions, in this order

Week 3 ended with a rule: chain, then process, then tool. Here is what each layer actually asks.

Layer 1 · The value chain

Where should I look?

A business is not one thing that makes money. It is a set of named activities, each with a cost and a contribution.

Gets you to the right neighborhood.

Layer 2 · The process

How does this actually run?

One activity, opened up: input, process, output, feedback. Who does each step, and where does the work sit waiting?

Gets you to the right step.

Layer 3 · The tool

Can a machine do it?

A scenario in Make. A trigger, some modules, a result. This is the layer everyone wants to start at.

Only useful once the first two are answered.

Skip a layer and you automate something nobody needed, faster. That is not a hypothetical — it is the most common way organizations waste money on automation.

The as-is map

Before you improve anything, draw what actually happens — not what the policy says happens. Four columns, and no formal notation required.

Column 1

Step

What happens, and who does it. One line each, in the order they really occur.

Column 2

Handoff

Where it goes next, and to whom. Every handoff is a place work can stop.

Column 3

Wait

How long it sits before anyone touches it. Be honest — use real times, not target times.

Column 4

System

Where it lives. A form, a sheet, an inbox, a binder, or somebody's memory.

Three rules make the difference between a map that teaches you something and a map that flatters you.

1
Draw it from memory first, then check it against reality. You will be wrong. The gap between what you believe happens and what happens is the finding — usually a step nobody mentioned, an approval routinely skipped, or a spreadsheet somebody maintains privately.
2
Follow one real item end to end. Not “how do invoices work,” but “what happened to invoice 4471.” Averages smooth out exactly the delays you are hunting for.
3
Mark every wait. Waits are where the time goes, and they sit between people rather than inside them. Delay between two owners is nobody's problem, which is why it survives for years.

Delete, then simplify, then automate

In that order, every time. The order is the whole lesson, because it is the one people reverse.

1

Delete

The step exists because of an incident nobody remembers. Removing it costs nothing and saves everything downstream.

Nothing breaks if we stop.

2

Simplify

Something breaks, but the step is bigger than the risk it manages. Shrink it before you build anything around it.

Something breaks, but less than the step costs.

3

Automate

Something real breaks and the step is genuinely needed. Now it is worth building — and only now.

Something real breaks.

Automating a step you could have deleted is the most expensive option on the list. You have paid to preserve something you should have removed — and now it is fast, cheap, and permanent, so nobody will ever revisit it.

Ask of every step: what breaks if we stop doing this? The answers sort themselves into the three groups above, and the sort is most of the analysis.

Where the machine stops

The design decision that separates a useful automation from an expensive one. Not every step a person performs is a step that requires one.

Give it to the machine

When all of this is true

✓
The answer is always derivable from the data
✓
The same input must give the same output
✓
It happens often enough to be worth building
✓
A mistake is visible and cheap to reverse
Keep the human

When any of this is true

!
It needs context the system does not hold
!
The answer depends on judgment or relationship
!
It is rare, and each case is genuinely different
!
A mistake is expensive or hard to detect

Do not automate the judgment. Automate everything around it, so the person spends their attention only on the judgment. Nobody is replaced, and the work moves faster.

A loan reviewer's day is forty percent judgment and sixty percent clerical — pulling records, checking completeness, assembling summaries, routing by size, notifying the applicant. Automate that sixty percent and the same person handles twice the volume, doing only the part that needed them. That is the shape you are looking for in every case.

Two traps to watch for

Both of these will show up in the casebook, and both catch people who understand the framework perfectly well.

Trap 1

High stakes is not the same as human judgment

A consequential step feels like it needs a person. But the real question is narrower: is the answer derivable from available data? Insurance eligibility matters enormously and is still a lookup with one correct answer.

Trap 2

A step involving a person is not always a step requiring one

“Route to the manager if it's over $500” is arithmetic wearing the costume of a decision. If the rule is written down, it belongs to the machine — no matter who currently performs it.
