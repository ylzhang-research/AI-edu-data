Before You Turn It On

Six questions about the automation you are proposing. They produce a risk tier, the controls that tier actually needs, and a draft of the risk section of your project proposal.

Automating a decision does not remove the accountability for it

When a person routes a complaint and gets it wrong, somebody answers for it. When a scenario routes it and gets it wrong, somebody still answers for it — the question is only whether anybody has worked out who, in advance, while it was still a choice rather than an incident.

The assumption

The system did it

Comfortable, and useless. No regulator, customer, auditor or board has ever accepted it, and neither will the person on the other end of the phone.

What is actually true

Accountability moved; it did not vanish

It landed on somebody. Usually on whoever built the thing, which is both unfair and fragile, because they rarely have the authority to change it.

This is not an ethics seminar. It is the operations question of who is answerable, what they are entitled to see, and what would make you stop.

Governance should be proportionate

The instinct when risk comes up is to add process to everything. That kills small automations as effectively as neglect kills large ones. The useful move is to tier by consequence and spend your governance where it matters — which is how regulators approach it too. The EU AI Act sorts systems into four bands from minimal risk up to banned, and classifies as high risk the cases that “can pose serious risks to health, safety or fundamental rights” — its own examples being CV-sorting software for recruitment and credit scoring. Your four tiers are the same idea scaled to a process you would actually run.

Most of what you build in this course is tier one or two, and the right answer there is a named owner, a note, and an error handler that tells somebody. Proposing a governance board for a file-renaming scenario is a mistake in the same family as proposing nothing for a hiring screen.
