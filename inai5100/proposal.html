The Proposal

Six short sections about the process you want to automate. Answer the questions, watch the paragraphs assemble, then download a Word file and submit it. This is the Final Project Proposal — 10 points, due Sunday, November 15.

What a proposal is for

It is not a description of a project and it is not an argument for one. It is a short document that lets one person decide, without a meeting. Everything below is written to that standard: specific enough to picture, honest enough to act on, and short enough to read in four minutes.

What loses marks

A description with adjectives

A process nobody can picture, a saving with no disposition, a risk section of imagined hazards, and a scope that would take a team a quarter.

What earns them

Something a reader could act on

A real process with numbers that have provenance, one honest claim, two named limits, a number that could prove you wrong, and a person who answers for it.

Five of these six sections already exist in work you have done. Section six comes out of this week’s lab. This page is assembly, not invention — which is the point.

Before you start

Pick the process deliberately. Everything downstream follows from it, and changing it in Week 14 is expensive. You want one you actually know — your employer, a non-profit you work with, a business you own, a department you have worked in — that you can scope down to a portion one person can finish. If your Weeks 10–11 business case and your Mini-Project 4 scenario were about the same process, you are most of the way there already.

Nobody has to fund or build this. You are writing something a real decision maker could act on if they chose to.
