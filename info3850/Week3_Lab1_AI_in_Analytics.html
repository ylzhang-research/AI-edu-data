Welcome

Why this lab?

In Monday's lecture we defined AI and its components (machine learning, NLP, computer vision, robotics, expert systems), placed AI inside the analytics continuum — descriptive → predictive → prescriptive — and looked at how businesses actually use analytics: fraud detection, forecasting and operations, customer analytics, and personalization.

This lab puts you in the analyst's seat. In each case you will read the data (descriptive), see what an AI model predicts (predictive), and then make a business decision with the model's help (prescriptive). Along the way you will meet the challenges from the lecture: false alarms, uncertainty, ROI, and privacy.

Case 1 · Riverbend Bank
Card-fraud detection. You set the alert threshold and manage the trade-off between missed fraud and annoyed customers.
Case 2 · Magnolia Market
Demand forecasting for a retailer. You compare forecasting methods and choose an order quantity under uncertainty.
Case 3 · StreamLine
Customer churn and personalization. You spend a retention budget where the AI says it will do the most good.
Learning objectives

By the end of this lab you can…

  • Identify which AI component (ML, NLP, etc.) is doing the work in a business analytics application.
  • Classify an analytics task as descriptive, predictive, or prescriptive by the question it answers.
  • Explain why a prediction is not a decision — and what a business must add (costs, constraints, judgment) to act on one.
  • Recognize the practical challenges of AI-driven analytics: false positives, forecast uncertainty, ROI justification, privacy, and cultural resistance.

Case 1 · Customer & fraud analytics

Riverbend Bank: catching card fraud without annoying customers

DescriptivePredictivePrescriptiveAI component: Machine Learning (classification)

Riverbend Bank processes about 2 million card transactions a day. Roughly 1 in 500 is fraudulent. A fraud analyst cannot read 2 million records, so the bank trained a machine-learning model on three years of historical transactions that were later confirmed as legitimate or fraudulent. The model learned patterns — unusual amounts, merchant categories, distance from home, time of day — and now assigns every new transaction a fraud score from 0 to 100.

Your job: decide the threshold. Any transaction scoring above it is declined and the customer gets a text message. Too low and honest customers are blocked at the checkout; too high and fraud slips through.

This is why the lecture said the goal of analytics is decision support, not decision replacement.

Case 2 · Operational analytics

Magnolia Market: forecasting demand and ordering inventory

DescriptivePredictivePrescriptiveAI component: Machine Learning (time-series / regression)

Magnolia Market is a regional home-and-garden retailer. Its best-selling fall item is the Heritage Garden Tote. The buyer has to place next month's order with the supplier four weeks in advance. Order too many and the extra totes sit in the warehouse; order too few and customers leave empty-handed and buy from a competitor.

Until last year the buyer ordered "about what we sold last time, plus a little." Now the company has an AI forecasting tool. Your job: compare the forecasting methods, then decide how many totes to order.

Backtest: we pretend we are at week 8, forecast weeks 9–12 with each method, and measure the average miss (mean absolute error). Lower is better. This is how analysts choose between models before trusting them.

Prescriptive · What should we do?

Choose the order quantity

Forecasts are never exact. The AI tool reports that actual demand typically lands between 85% and 115% of its forecast. Each unsold tote costs $2 to hold and clear; each unit of unmet demand costs $9 in lost margin and goodwill.

The optimizer is only as good as the costs you gave it — if the $9 stockout cost is wrong, the "optimal" order is wrong. Case 3 · Customer analytics & personalization

StreamLine: keeping the subscribers who are about to leave

DescriptivePredictivePrescriptiveAI components: Machine Learning + NLP

StreamLine is a $14.99/month video-streaming service with 1.2 million subscribers. Every month about 4.5% cancel ("churn"). Winning a new subscriber costs about $60 in advertising, so keeping an existing one is far cheaper. The retention team has a budget for $20 "stay with us" offers (one free month plus a personalized recommendation list), but not enough to send one to everybody.

Two AI models help. A churn model (machine learning) scores each subscriber's probability of cancelling next month. A text model (natural language processing) reads support chats and reviews to detect frustration before it shows up in the numbers.

Think about it: the customer with the highest churn probability is not always the best target. Some people will leave no matter what (low uplift); others were never going to leave (the offer is wasted). Personalization means matching the action to the person — the lecture's personalization application.
Privacy and ethics. StreamLine is reading customers' chats and viewing habits. Under the lecture's challenges this is a security and privacy concern: the company must disclose what it collects, secure it, and avoid using sensitive attributes in ways that discriminate. Regulators (GDPR, CCPA) and customers both punish companies that get this wrong.
